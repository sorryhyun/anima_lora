CJK Renderable Anima

Anima already holds Japanese glyph units in its weights; it garbles them because the T5-side query stream never gave them an address. This line trains only that address — one free vector fr per vocab-pack ext row — through a DiT, adapter and text encoder that never move, on scene composites the base model drew itself. Since 2026-09-17 the supervision is paired: every composite is drawn twice, once with the new glyph and once with a string the base already renders, and the row is trained on the difference. What ships is the pack, with the trained rows baked in.

The loss: a paired difference, not a plain residual

The line pays for identity in exposure — ≈ 1 000 composite draws per row — and every shortcut to the address itself is closed (shape encoder, composition, transplant, warm start; last section). So this changes the other term: the supervision per draw. It was proposed as an exposure saver and is not one — at equal draws ΔFM ties plain FM and needs twice the learning rate to get there, because the part it cancels is the sign-consistent part Adam travels on. What it buys is a table that renders inside a scene instead of wiping it: en cos 0.93 against 0.855, images below 0.85 six of 64 against twenty. The open cost is the other side of the same coin — with the wipe gone, the base free-runs JA pseudo-text beside the addressed glyph, which is what the sentence step is being run to test.

Where it lands: the frozen text path

Both branches of the pair run through this, with the same weights and the same σ. It is the text side of a frozen Anima: the one object that moves is the ext-row table the T5 id stream reads, and it moves only through branch B's caption — branch A's holds no ext id and runs detached.

The address, written out

The only thing trained is the ext-row table Ẽ that llm_adapter.embed reads. The recipe of record is rows-only: no shared vector, no glyph encoder, no quote direction at train time — each of those was measured and removed (table below) — and, since Δ1, paired: --pair_ref en --pair_loss 1 at --lr_rows 2e-3, flat items 0, every row drawn at the same weight. How it is trained: pack → step 1 → step 2 → bake

One table, grown in stages, each stage warm-starting from the last. The pack supplies the ids and a row per id; step 1 binds single glyphs, under the paired loss since Δ1; step 2 binds punctuation, words and sentence order on the same rows; the bake writes the sum back into an ordinary pack. What passes from stage to stage is F (trained.pt) and nothing else — no DiT or adapter weight ever changes. Whether the paired loss survives step 2 is the open question of the line: its one measured cost is JA pseudo-text beside the addressed glyph, and a caption that is entirely trained rows leaves nothing unaddressed to invent — if that holds, the cost disappears exactly where the sentence target lives.

What it sees, what it draws

Every item is a scene composite: the base model draws a character scene with an English speech bubble (or sign), the anchor text is erased with the bubble's own colour, and the Japanese text is drawn into the region in one of 16 manga faces, the anchor's ink colour, 30 % tilted. The caption is the scene's own tags with english text → japanese text plus the same frame the scene was drawn under (reads as / speech bubble that reads / She is saying / holding a sign that reads). Under the paired loss each is drawn twice (top figure). Flat font bubbles are 0: they were 10 % of the plain-FM recipe, but a flat item has no scene to preserve and, as built, a flat batch falls back to unscaled plain FM — a loss 5–8× the paired one, sharing Adam's moments with the composite steps and pushing exactly the bare-canvas component the pairing cancels. The renders at the bottom are the earlier plain-FM table (433 rows, ≈ 490 draws per row), read by the SFX OCR reader.

Why a row, not a script

A trained row is an address, not a shape coordinate. Held-out kana stay at 2–5/64 whatever a glyph encoder does, so identity lives in fr and every row needs its own exposure — the measured curve is 1 330 draws per row → 100 % singles, 670 → 75 %, 490 → 36 %; ≈ 1 000 draws per row is the planning number, and the 53k run's words scored 0/32 because the item sampler gave them ≈ 8 each.

The 53k table, read in row space and at the adapter output: one shared trigger direction (18 % of the energy, orthogonal to the pretrained quote direction under every frame) plus near-orthogonal per-row residuals — no manifold to amortise. Flat-trained identity does not transfer onto the composite trigger (0/64 for two flat donors); composite-trained identity does (23/64); inheriting the trigger buys no steps. Composite exposure per row is the cost.

A row can be a whole word — Qwen merges します or ありがとう into one piece and the DiT draws it as one unit — but a sequence of trained rows renders one unit, while the same frozen stack reads pretrained EN piece sequences in order (nonsense words 22/24). That is the sentence line: Manga109 dialogue (40 446 lines of 3–10 pieces, held out by book) drawn into sentence-anchored scenes, continuing from the 53k table. Two blockers before the arm: the composite draw is single-line, so a 40 px glyph cell caps most bubbles at 2–4 glyphs (wide 576–640 px canvases and 2–3-line wrapping are the fixes), and the phrase share must be pinned, since it is the only exposure the punctuation rows get.

Why it is hard: the row → glyph map is a hash

Every shortcut to f that was tried assumes the trained rows carry some structure a glyph's shape could predict — smoothness, linearity, a metric, a shared codebook. Measured in the 53k table, none is there. The frozen adapter + DiT reads a row as one unit and any near-orthogonal direction will do, so the only thing that binds a direction to a glyph is the gradient the DiT sends back while it draws that glyph — exposure.

What each shortcut assumed, and what it measured. Every one was measured under plain flow matching through the frozen DiT (embedding inversion is that loss with one image); the levers differ only in what structure they impose on F. The paired loss is not on this list and does not reopen it: it changes the supervision per draw, not the row → glyph map, and the rows it trains are random-init and near-orthogonal exactly as before.
